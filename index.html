The verdict in one line

The problem is not missing trust signals. It is trust signals that contradict each other.

This page has real assets: 1,038 reviews at 4.79, genuine on-face customer photos, a nine-image gallery, a sticky add-to-cart, and a Made in Korea pack. But it also tells the buyer the collagen is vegan while the packet in the hero says it is New Zealand origin, presents a "Seal of Trust" certificate issued to a different company, and stays completely silent on returns while the policy page quietly says refunds are never paid in money. A skeptical buyer catches one of these, then discounts everything else on the page to zero. Four of the five barriers below are copy and asset governance, not design spend. The fifth is a third-party app audit.

01 P1 B04 Authenticity B44 Reaction Risk Missing

The page says the collagen is vegan. The packet says it is from New Zealand.

"You're telling me it's vegan collagen, but the packet in your own hero photo says 100% New Zealand origin collagen. Which one is the lie?" The doubt in her head
What is happening

New Zealand origin collagen is marine or bovine. It cannot be vegan. Both claims are visible within two thumb-scrolls of each other on mobile: the badge sits on the pack in the first hero slide, the card sits in the Key Ingredients row further down. The long-form copy at the bottom then compounds it by recommending snail mucin as a key ingredient, which is animal-derived and is not in this formula at all.

Evidence
Compounding issue: the "Full Ingredient List" is not an INCI list. It reads Collagen, Vitamin c, Hyaluronic Acid, Glycerin, Niacinamide, Vitamin E, Centella, Vitamin B5, Organic, Hyaluronic acid, Vegan. Hyaluronic acid appears twice, and "Organic" and "Vegan" are listed as if they were ingredients.
Why it ranks first

This is the barrier a skeptical buyer catches within seconds, and once caught it retroactively discounts every other trust claim on the page. The Korean positioning, the certificate, the 1,038 reviews, the panel stats: all of it gets reweighted downward by a buyer who has just proven to herself that the page will say two incompatible things about the single ingredient the product is named after.

02 P2 B04 Authenticity Missing

The "Seal of Trust" certificate belongs to a different company.

"This certificate names ELAIROSE and SAVK Ventures, not Wildglow, and it's stamped by a company in Guangzhou. Where is this actually made?" The doubt in her head
What is happening

The PDP carries a "View Certificate" link that opens a full-screen test report watermarked SEAL OF TRUST. Read it and four separate problems surface at once.

Reported to    M/s. ELAIROSE, SAVK Ventures Private Limited, Mumbai  not Wildglow Contact           Jane Chang, yalanhk@foxmail.com  consumer mail domain Approved by    Guangzhou Yalan International Cosmetics Ltd.  China, on a Korean-positioned product Test method    Melanin inhibition, 0.78% to 1.56%  supports no claim on this page
Evidence
Why it ranks second

Most buyers will not open it. But the ones who do are exactly the high-intent, high-scrutiny buyers who convert at the top of the funnel and who leave reviews. For that segment this document does not build trust, it destroys it, because it simultaneously suggests the product is white-labelled from a Chinese manufacturer, sold under a different brand elsewhere, and certified for a property nobody was asking about. An unverifiable certificate is worse than no certificate, because it converts a neutral gap into an active red flag.

03 B08 Risk Reversal B69 Total Cost B10 Payment Missing

Zero returns, shipping or COD information on the PDP. And the policy behind it says refunds are never paid in money.

"What if it doesn't work, or never turns up? Can I even pay cash on delivery? When does it arrive?" The doubt in her head
What is happening

The mobile PDP contains no occurrence of COD, cash on delivery, shipping, free shipping threshold, delivery ETA, returns, refunds or guarantee. Below the Add to Cart button there is nothing at all. On an Indian D2C page where a buyer may be committing up to ₹5,426, this is the largest unforced omission on the page.

Evidence
The cross-check

The linked returns policy is not just hidden, it is internally contradictory, and the version a buyer would actually experience is far harsher than anything the PDP implies.

Policy page saysVerbatim
CancellationsRefunds are issued as a lifetime coupon code. "Refunds to bank accounts are not applicable."
Pre-dispatch refunds"Refunds will not be issued instead, you will receive a coupon code of the same value."
Damaged productRequires an unboxing video within 24 to 48 hours. "No returns or replacements will be accepted without video proof."
Returns informationContradicts all of the above: "the amount of your order will be refunded to you within 7 business days." Also sets a different 3-day window, and reserves final discretion to the brand.
This is already visible to buyers. A review sitting in the on-page feed reads: "Odar kidhar hai mera or return ke pese ku nhi aaye abhi tak" (where is my order and why has the refund money not come). It has no brand reply. That is the coupon-only policy showing up as public evidence, on the PDP, unaddressed.
Why it ranks third

Baymard has had unexpected cost and unclear returns as the top abandonment drivers for six consecutive years. Here the cost is not just unclear, the recovery path is genuinely poor, and the buyer will find that out after paying. Surfacing an honest version is the fix, but the policy has to be fixed first, because publishing the current one on the PDP would suppress conversion further.

04 P3 P4 B05 Value B15 Selection Missing

Five pack options with no prices on them, and the page never says what a pack contains.

"Pack of 1 what? One sachet, one night? And is Pack of 4 actually cheaper per mask, or just more money?" The doubt in her head
What is happening

The variant selector shows five bare pills. No price, no per-mask price, no savings figure, no "best value" marker. On mobile the buyer has to tap each one and watch the price above change to discover the ladder exists. Most will not. Separately, nowhere on the page does it state how many masks or how many nights a pack contains. At ₹399 the difference between one use and ten is the entire buying decision.

Two problems, not one. The ladder is invisible, and the ladder is also weak: buying sixteen units instead of one saves only 15 percent. There is no reason on the page for anyone to move up.

The offers also cannibalise each other. This SKU sits in the site's Buy 2 Get 1 Free collection, linked from the top nav. Three units for ₹798 works out to ₹266 per mask, materially better than Pack of 16 at ₹339 per mask. Neither offer acknowledges the other, and the PDP surfaces neither the comparison nor the better deal.
Why it ranks fourth

The trust barriers above protect conversion rate. This one is the AOV lever, and it is the cheapest structural win available on the page. Everything needed to fix it already exists in the product data.

The 45 to 65 gap is not a rebuild. It is a third-party app audit. A Lighthouse mobile run against the live URL finds 2,350 KB of unused JavaScript, five separate Google tag containers loading in parallel, two competing one-click checkout providers on the same PDP, and a 1,808 KB Amazon widget that is the single heaviest item on the page. None of that is design or content.
Why it ranks fifth

It ranks below the trust barriers because a fast page that contradicts itself still does not convert, and because speed suppresses conversion quietly rather than blocking it outright. But it ranks above everything else in the grading, because it is the only barrier on this list that also taxes paid acquisition: every second of load is spent on traffic Wildglow has already paid for.
